2.8Basic network design and structured cabling

  • Plan for scalability, reliability, security and cost.
  • A common enterprise design has three layers: access (end devices connect), distribution (policy, routing between VLANs) and core (fast backbone). Small networks collapse these into one or two layers.
  • Use structured cabling: work area, horizontal cabling, patch panel, equipment room, backbone.

The four design goals

  • Scalability: the network can grow without being redesigned. Leave spare switch ports, spare space in racks and cable trays, and spare IP addresses.
  • Reliability (availability): important parts have a backup, so one failure does not stop everyone. Examples: two links from each access switch to the distribution layer, two power supplies, a UPS (uninterruptible power supply).
  • Security: separate users into VLANs (see 3.2.4), protect the edge with a firewall, and restrict who can manage the devices.
  • Cost: meet today's needs and allow for sensible growth, without buying far more than is needed.

The three layers explained

  • Access layer: where users plug in. Switches with many ports in each wiring closet, plus wireless access points. Features: VLANs, port security, Power over Ethernet (PoE) for phones, cameras and access points.
  • Distribution layer: collects the uplinks from access switches. It routes between VLANs, applies policies (which networks can talk to which), and provides redundant links.
  • Core layer: the high-speed backbone joining the distribution switches, the server room and the Internet connection. Its only job is to move traffic as fast and reliably as possible.

A small school or office usually uses a collapsed core: one pair of switches does both the distribution and core jobs.

Structured cabling

Structured cabling is a standard way of organising the cables, so that changes are easy and faults are quick to find:

  • Work area: the wall socket and patch cord at the user's desk.
  • Horizontal cabling: the fixed cable from the wall socket to the wiring closet on the same floor (up to 90 m).
  • Telecommunications room (wiring closet): the patch panels and access switches for that floor.
  • Backbone (vertical) cabling: usually fibre, between floors and buildings.
  • Equipment room: the main switches, servers and the Internet connection.

Label both ends of every cable and every patch panel port, and keep the records up to date.

The design process

  1. Gather requirements: the number of users and devices, the applications, wireless coverage, security needs, budget and expected growth.
  2. Survey the site: the building layout, cable routes, power, and where to put wiring closets and access points.
  3. Design: the topology, equipment list, IP addressing plan (see Unit 4) and VLANs.
  4. Implement: install, configure and test.
  5. Document: diagrams, IP addresses, device passwords (stored securely) and labels.
  6. Monitor and improve: watch performance and plan upgrades before problems appear.

Remember

  • Design for scalability, reliability, security and cost.
  • Access, distribution and core layers; small networks collapse the top two.
  • Structured cabling and good labelling make changes and troubleshooting easy.
